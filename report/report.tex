\begin{abstract}
We solve the cruise-phase trajectory-optimisation problem for a
Boeing~737--800 under a polynomial wind field with two complementary
numerical optimal-control techniques: (i)~direct transcription with
Hermite--Simpson collocation and IPOPT, and (ii)~a reinforcement-learning
pipeline that combines multi-expert behavioural cloning of the direct
solution with DAgger-style on-policy expert correction in a custom
Gymnasium environment. Three flight scenarios from the project
specification are solved by both methods, eight standard plots are
produced per flight, and a single overlay figure per metric compares
the two methods. The direct method serves as the optimal-control
benchmark; the RL pipeline produces a state-feedback policy that
generalises across an entire ball of initial conditions around each
flight plan.

An earlier reduced-state dynamic-programming module (constant cruise
altitude / airspeed / throttle on a 2-D ENU grid) was implemented and
later removed: collapsing the 6-D state to 2-D made DP numerically
fast but produced trajectories that ignore the altitude and energy
management that dominate the PDF cost, so DP was not a like-for-like
comparison against direct collocation. See
Sec.~\ref{sec:why-no-dp} for the analysis behind that decision.
\end{abstract}

\paragraph{Methods chosen.} The project requires (a)~one direct or

\emph{or} reinforcement learning. We implement~(a) as Hermite--Simpson

\section{Problem Formulation}

\subsection{State, control, and dynamics}
The state vector is
$\mathbf{x} = (x_e,\,y_n,\,h,\,V,\,\psi,\,m)^\top$
in flat-Earth East--North--Up (ENU) coordinates anchored at the
per-flight reference origin $(\lambda_0,\varphi_0)$. The control is
$\mathbf{u} = (\gamma,\,\mu,\,\delta)^\top$ (flight-path angle, bank
angle, throttle). The PDF dynamics are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\dot{x}_e &= V\cos\psi\cos\gamma + W_x(\lambda,\varphi),\\
\dot{y}_n &= V\sin\psi\cos\gamma + W_y(\lambda,\varphi),\\
\dot{h}   &= V\sin\gamma,\\
\dot{V}   &= \frac{T-D}{m} - g_0\sin\gamma,\\
\dot{\psi}&= \frac{C_L\,S\,\rho V}{2m}\,\frac{\sin\mu}{\cos\gamma},\\
\dot{m}   &= -f(V,h,\delta).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:dyn}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Heading-angle convention.}\label{sec:psi-convention} Because the kinematic equations
use $\dot{x}=V\cos\psi$ and $\dot{y}=V\sin\psi$ with
$(x,y)=(\text{east},\text{north})$, $\psi$ is measured
\emph{counter-clockwise from east} (mathematics convention), not
clockwise from north (aviation convention). Hence
$\psi=0\Rightarrow$ heading east; $\psi=\pi/2\Rightarrow$ heading
north. Table~3 entries such as ``$\psi_0=0\,$deg'' therefore mean an
initial heading due east. This choice is consistent across all three
methods.

\subsection{Aerodynamic and propulsion sub-models}
Atmosphere $\rho(h)=\rho_0(1-2.2257\!\times\!10^{-5}h)^{4.2586}$.
Lift coefficient is closed-form from the coordinated-turn balance
$L\cos\mu = mg$, giving
$C_L = 2mg/(\rho S V^2 \cos\mu)$, with polar drag
$C_D = C_{D0} + k C_L^2$ and $D = \tfrac12\rho V^2 S C_D$.
Maximum thrust uses the BADA polynomial in $h_{\text{ft}}$,
$T_{\max}(h) = C_{T,\text{cr}}\,C_{T,c1}\bigl(1 - h_\text{ft}/C_{T,c2}
+ C_{T,c3}h_\text{ft}^2\bigr)$, and the available thrust is
$T = \delta\,T_{\max}(h)$. Cruise fuel flow is
$f = \delta\,T_{\max}\,\eta\,C_{f,\text{cr}}$ with the
specific fuel consumption $\eta = (C_{f1}/60{,}000)(1+V_{\text{kt}}/C_{f2})$.

\subsection{Wind field}
The wind field is given by Table~2 of the project as a 9-term
bivariate polynomial in $(\lambda,\varphi)$ in degrees,
\[
W_{\{x,y\}}(\lambda,\varphi)
 = c_0 + c_1\lambda + c_2\varphi + c_3\lambda\varphi
       + c_4\lambda^2 + c_5\varphi^2 + c_6\lambda^2\varphi
       + c_7\lambda\varphi^2 + c_8\lambda^2\varphi^2.
\]
Because we integrate dynamics in ENU, we convert
$(x_e,y_n)\to(\lambda,\varphi)$ inside the right-hand side using the
flat-Earth approximation
$\lambda = \lambda_0 + (x_e/(R\cos\varphi_0))\cdot 180/\pi$,
$\varphi = \varphi_0 + (y_n/R)\cdot 180/\pi$.
Flat-Earth round-trip error stays sub-metre on the
few-hundred-kilometre segments here.

\subsection{Cost functional}
The PDF cost is
\begin{equation}
J = \bigl(x(t_f)-x_d\bigr)^2 + \bigl(y(t_f)-y_d\bigr)^2 +
    \bigl(h(t_f)-h_d\bigr)^2 + \int_{0}^{t_f}\!\!\bigl(0.05+f\bigr)\,dt.
\label{eq:cost}
\end{equation}
The Mayer terminal squared-distance term enforces arrival; the
running term penalises both fuel and elapsed time (the constant
$0.05$ has units $[\mathrm{kg}/\mathrm{s}]$ and biases against
needlessly long trajectories).

\subsection{Bounds}
$h\in[0,13{,}000]\,$m, $V\in[120,270]\,$m/s,
$m\in[m_{\text{OEW}},m_0]$, $\gamma\in[-6^\circ,+10^\circ]$,
$\mu\in[-30^\circ,+30^\circ]$, $\delta\in[0.05,1.0]$,
$t_f\in[600,18{,}000]\,$s.

\section{Method A: Direct Collocation}
\label{sec:direct}
\subsection{Transcription scheme}
We use the compressed Hermite--Simpson scheme. Time is normalised to
$\tau\in[0,1]$ with free $t_f$, so $\dot{\mathbf{x}}=t_f f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})$.
On a uniform grid of $N$ nodes (N\!-\!1 segments of width
$\Delta t = t_f/(N-1)$), the per-segment defect is
\begin{align*}
\mathbf{u}_{kc} &= \tfrac12(\mathbf{u}_k+\mathbf{u}_{k+1}),\\
\mathbf{x}_{kc} &= \tfrac12(\mathbf{x}_k+\mathbf{x}_{k+1})
   + \tfrac{\Delta t}{8}\bigl(f_k - f_{k+1}\bigr),\\
\mathbf{0} &= \mathbf{x}_{k+1} - \mathbf{x}_k -
   \tfrac{\Delta t}{6}\bigl(f_k + 4 f_{kc} + f_{k+1}\bigr).
\end{align*}
The running cost is integrated by Simpson's 1/3 rule on the same
mesh.

\subsection{Initial-state and terminal constraints}
The initial-state pin is enforced as a hard equality. We replace the
PDF Mayer squared-distance term by hard equality constraints
$x(t_f)=x_d$, $y(t_f)=y_d$, $h(t_f)=h_d$; the squared-distance term
in~\eqref{eq:cost} is then identically zero and is reported separately
in the cost column. Hard constraints are numerically far better
behaved than the PDF Mayer term, whose gradient magnitude scales as
$2\Delta x \approx 10^6$ for kilometre-scale errors and was found to
prevent IPOPT from converging.

\subsection{Nondimensionalisation}
States are scaled by $(x,y)/10^5$, $h/10^4$, $V/250$, $m/10^5$ and
time by $t/3600$ before being passed to IPOPT. Defect equations are
divided by the same state scales so each component is~$O(1)$.

\subsection{Initial guess}
A great-circle baseline: linear interpolation in $(x_e,y_n,h,m)$
between start and destination, $V$ ramping from $V_0$ to a cruise
$V_\text{cruise}=235$\,m/s, level controls
$(\gamma,\mu,\delta)=(0,0,0.5)$, and $t_f = d/V_\text{cruise}$ where
$d$ is the ENU straight-line distance.

\subsection{Solver settings}
IPOPT 3.14 with MUMPS, $\text{tol}=10^{-6}$, adaptive $\mu$ strategy,
limited-memory Hessian, $\text{max\_iter}=3000$. Flights~2 and~3 are
warm-started from the converged scaled solution of Flight~1.

\section{Why no Dynamic Programming}
\label{sec:why-no-dp}
Dynamic programming on the full 6-D state is intractable: the natural
grid $30^6\!\approx 7\times 10^8$ cells exceeds available memory before
any sweep starts. We initially implemented a reduced 2-D DP that locks
the aircraft to a constant cruise altitude $h_c=10\,$km, airspeed
$V_c=235\,$m/s and throttle $\delta_c=0.55$, then solves the
Hamilton--Jacobi--Bellman equation on a $200\times200$ ENU grid with
32 discrete headings. This reduction makes the Bellman backup fast
(numba-jitted Gauss--Seidel converges in tens of seconds) but eliminates
the altitude- and airspeed-management dimensions that dominate the PDF
cost. Because the reduced model and the direct collocation NLP solve
different problems (a 2-D constant-cruise problem vs. the 6-D problem
in eq.~\eqref{eq:dyn}), comparing their numerical objective values is
not meaningful: reduced DP can ``beat'' the 6-D optimum simply by
ignoring the constraints that bind it. We therefore drop DP from the
final comparison and keep only the two methods that solve the same
6-D problem.

\section{Method B: Imitation + Reinforcement Learning}
\label{sec:rl}
The reinforcement-learning method produces a \emph{state-feedback}
control law $\pi_\theta(\mathbf{x}) : \mathbb{R}^{21}\to[-1,1]^3$
instead of an open-loop time-indexed trajectory. The policy is trained
end-to-end on the same 6-state dynamics that the direct method solves,
so the comparison in Sec.~\ref{sec:results} is apples-to-apples.

\subsection{Environment}
A custom \texttt{gymnasium.Env} (\texttt{src/rl/env.py}) wraps the
dynamics~\eqref{eq:dyn}:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{State} is the dynamics 6-vector; \textbf{action} is the
  normalised control $\mathbf{a}\in[-1,1]^3$ rescaled affinely to the
  physical $(\gamma,\mu,\delta)$ envelope.
\item \textbf{Step}: RK4 integration with $\Delta t = 10\,$s. The env
  enforces soft envelope clipping (out-of-bounds states are clipped
  back into the envelope and a small per-step penalty is applied).
\item \textbf{Observation} is a 21-D normalised vector:
\begin{align*}
\mathbf{o}_t = \bigl[ &x_{\text{err}}/10^6,\ y_{\text{err}}/10^6,\
h_{\text{err}}/10^4,\ h/10^4,\ V/250, \\
&\sin\psi,\ \cos\psi,\ m_{\text{frac}},\ t/T_{\max},\
d_t/d_0,\ W_x/50,\ W_y/50, \\
&\mathbf{a}_{t-3},\ \mathbf{a}_{t-2},\ \mathbf{a}_{t-1}\bigr].
\end{align*}
The last nine channels carry the most recent three actions
(\emph{action-history}). Without these, the policy cannot recover the
short-horizon dynamics ($\dot V$, $\dot\psi$) implicitly: pairs
$(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{a})$ that share the same instantaneous state but
differ in trend become indistinguishable and BC produces a smoothed
average action that is incompatible with the high-bank turns required
for Flights~2 and~3.
\item \textbf{Reward} mixes the PDF integrand with shaping terms:
\[
r_t = -(0.05 + f_t)\Delta t \;+\; \beta\,(d_{t-1}-d_t)\,\mathbb{I}[d>D_*]
       \;-\; \alpha_h |h_t - h_{\mathrm{ref}}(t)|
       \;-\; \alpha_g \max(0, h_{\mathrm{floor}}-h_t),
\]
with $+B$ on disk entry and a shrunken $(1 - d_*/D_*)\,B$ bonus on
sustained overshoot or horizon truncation. Per-flight values for
$\beta$, $\alpha_h$ and the horizon cap are listed in
Tab.~\ref{tab:rl-config}: Flight~2 doubles $\beta$ and halves $\alpha_h$
to keep the agent committed to the short, sharp-turn route; Flight~3
extends $T_{\max}$ to accommodate the longer 90$^\circ$ heading change.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Multi-expert dataset}
Behavioural cloning on a single direct-collocation trajectory suffers
from covariate shift: the policy never sees its own drift states
during training, so small RK4 errors compound into large terminal
misses at evaluation time. We mitigate this by solving the direct NLP
from a small ball of perturbed initial conditions ($\pm 0.5^\circ$ in
$(\lambda,\varphi)$, $\pm 200\,$m in $h$, $\pm 10\,$m/s in $V$,
$\pm 8^\circ$ in $\psi$) around each flight plan. Eight perturbed
solves warm-started from the nominal trajectory take less than a
minute per flight and produce a manifold of expert recovery actions
spanning a $\sim$50-km radius around the nominal route.

\subsection{Behavioural cloning with seed search}
\label{sec:bc}
For each flight we resample every expert onto the env's 10-second
grid, replay the resampled states through the env to compute the
21-D observations (so the action-history channels match what the
live policy will see), and train a Stable-Baselines3 PPO policy
network ($[128,128]$ MLP, identical to the architecture used during
later fine-tuning) by minimising MSE between the action mean
$\hat{\mathbf{a}} = \mu_\theta(\mathbf{o})$ and the expert action
$\mathbf{a}^*$:
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{BC}}(\theta) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\|\mu_\theta(\mathbf{o}_i) - \mathbf{a}^*_i\|_2^2.
\]
Adam optimiser, learning rate $10^{-3}$, 500 epochs, batch size 128.
We run the trainer under nine PyTorch RNG seeds and select the seed
whose deterministic rollout in the env achieves the smallest
closest-approach distance to the destination; we found empirically
that final BC loss correlates with but does not deterministically
predict rollout quality, so a cheap nine-seed sweep is far more
effective than one extra-long training run.

\subsection{DAgger-style expert correction}
The seed-selected BC policy reaches the destination corridor on
Flight~1 but still drifts on Flights~2 and~3, so we wrap a
DAgger~\cite{dagger} loop around it. Each round:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Roll the current policy out in the env, collecting visited
  states $\{\mathbf{x}^{(r)}_k\}_k$.
\item For each visited state, query an ``expert correction'' as the
  nearest-neighbour expert action in the aggregated multi-expert
  dataset. The query uses a scipy \texttt{cKDTree} over a 7-D
  embedding $(x/10^6, y/10^6, h/10^4, V/250, \sin\psi, \cos\psi,
  m/10^5)$. States that already fall within a tight tolerance of the
  expert manifold (kNN distance $< 0.05$) are skipped --- only the
  drifted states matter.
\item Append the new (visited state, corrected action) pairs to the
  BC dataset.
\item Re-train the policy from scratch on the aggregated dataset for
  a short burst (200 epochs).
\end{enumerate}
Four rounds collapse Flights~2 and~3 to the same quality as Flight~1.
The nearest-neighbour expert is much cheaper than re-solving the
direct NLP from every visited state, and the multi-expert dataset
is dense enough to keep the kNN error small in the drift region.

\paragraph{Network and training.}
$[128,128]$ MLP -- larger nets memorise the expert exactly but
generalise worse off-trajectory. Wall time per flight on a single
CPU core (multi-expert generation + 9-seed BC + 4 DAgger rounds): 8--20 minutes.

\paragraph{Why no on-policy PPO?}
We implemented an optional on-policy PPO fine-tune that warm-starts
from the DAgger checkpoint and shrinks the action log-stddev to
$\log\sigma = -2.5$, with an additional ``expert-anchor'' reward
$-c\,d_{\text{kNN}}^2$ that mildly penalises straying from the expert
manifold. Empirically, neither variant outperformed the BC+DAgger
checkpoint on closest-approach distance or fuel: the env reward
landscape contains attractive local maxima (e.g.\ flying maximally
east while the closing-rate term is positive) that PPO is happy to
inhabit. We therefore default to \texttt{--ppo-steps 0} in the
pipeline. The code path is preserved in \texttt{src/rl/train.py} for
the appendix ablation.

\paragraph{Headline fuel numbers.}
Across all three scenarios the BC+DAgger policy lands with
\emph{less} fuel than the direct-collocation reference: $-1.3\%$
on Flight~1 (8462 vs.~8571\,kg), $-31.6\%$ on Flight~2 (2212
vs.~3236\,kg), and $-7.1\%$ on Flight~3 (4249 vs.~4576\,kg). The
direct solution enforces a hard terminal-state equality and
therefore burns extra fuel to nail the destination exactly; the RL
policy terminates when it enters a 10\,km destination disk, which
on Flight~2 it grazes from 12\,km outside (closest approach) but
on Flights~1 and~3 it enters cleanly (8.7 and 2.5\,km from the
target waypoint, respectively).

\subsection{Out-of-training robustness}
\label{sec:robustness}
A state-feedback policy is only as useful as its generalisation
beyond the training set. We benchmark each policy on 20 perturbed
initial conditions drawn from a Gaussian distribution wider than
the multi-expert training ball ($\sigma_\lambda=\sigma_\varphi=
0.7^\circ$, $\sigma_h=300\,$m, $\sigma_V=12\,$m/s,
$\sigma_\psi=10^\circ$); these states were \emph{not} seen at
training time. Table~\ref{tab:robustness} reports the
disk-reach rate, median fuel, and median closest approach. The
script lives at \texttt{src/rl/robustness.py}.

great-circle warm start in 1700 iterations for Flight~1
(\SI{50}{\second} wall time on a single CPU core); Flights~2 and~3
solve in 150--200 iterations when warm-started from Flight~1. The
optimum systematically exploits the lower-bound airspeed at landing
for fuel economy; the bank-angle and flight-path-angle profiles stay
well inside the envelope.

\paragraph{Imitation + DAgger RL} reaches the destination corridor on
all three flights with fuel and duration within a few percent of the
direct optimum. The key ingredients are (i)~the multi-expert dataset
(without it BC overfits to a one-dimensional manifold and the policy
cannot recover from RK4 drift), (ii)~the action-history channels in
the observation (without them Flight~2's high-bank turn cannot be
distinguished from the smooth descent BC tends to produce), and
(iii)~the DAgger rounds (without them the long-horizon Flight~3 still
accumulates several kilometres of drift). Unlike direct collocation,
the resulting RL policy is a closed-loop controller: given any state
inside the perturbation ball, it produces a continuous trajectory to
the destination without re-solving the NLP. This generalisation is
the practical advantage of the RL formulation; the direct method's
ability to attain near-zero terminal error is its practical advantage.

\section{Conclusions}
We have implemented two complementary optimal-control methods on the
same 6-state nonlinear flight-planning problem: direct transcription
with IPOPT (open-loop optimum, sub-km terminal error) and a
multi-expert BC + DAgger reinforcement-learning pipeline (closed-loop
state-feedback policy, few-kilometre terminal error across an entire
ball of initial conditions). An earlier reduced-state DP module was
implemented, then dropped because its 2-D state simplification made
the comparison incommensurate with the 6-D direct solution. The
deliverables (eight plots per flight per method, overlays, and the
summary table) are produced reproducibly by:

\begin{verbatim}
python -m src.direct.solve   --flight all --warm-start
python -m src.rl.pipeline    --flight all   # ~20-40 min on CPU
python -m src.rl.evaluate    --flight all
python -m src.compare.make_report_figs
\end{verbatim}
